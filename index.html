Austin R. Carter PhD, MPH

Research Scientist, IHME Consultant, World Health Organization Portland, OR

I build models that estimate what health programs have achieved and what they still could: deaths averted by vaccines, infections prevented by HIV interventions, and malaria outbreaks caught early enough to act on.

I am a Research Scientist at the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation at the University of Washington, where I have worked on HIV, malaria, and COVID-19 estimation since 2015. Since 2020 I have also consulted part-time for WHO on vaccine impact for Immunization Agenda 2030. My PhD in Health Metrics Sciences (UW, 2025) introduced sequential Shapley value estimation, a way to divide averted burden among interventions whose effects overlap in time.

Research

WHO · 2020 – present

Vaccine impact for Immunization Agenda 2030

14 pathogens · 194 countries · 2021–2030

I built and maintain the model WHO uses to estimate deaths averted by vaccination, which sets the targets for IA2030 Impact Goal 1.1 and tracks annual progress against them. I presented the methods, validation, and uncertainty analysis to WHO's IVIR-AC advisory committee at four meetings from 2021 to 2023.

Vaccine, 2024 The Lancet, 2024 Code: vieIA2030

IHME · PhD dissertation

Averted and avertable burden of pediatric HIV

4.8 M infections · 3.6 M deaths averted · sub-Saharan Africa, 2000–2023

Prevention of mother-to-child transmission, pediatric antiretroviral therapy, and cotrimoxazole prophylaxis were scaled up at different times, and their effects overlap. Sequential Shapley value estimation attributes the burden each one averted, country by country and year by year, and estimates how many deaths full coverage could still prevent. I am also lead author of IHME's Global Burden of Disease HIV papers.

Preprint, 2026 Dissertation, 2025 GBD HIV, Lancet HIV 2024 R package: shapleydecomp

IHME · Uganda

Malaria surveillance and outbreak detection in Uganda

DHIS2 facility reports → monthly district-level outbreak alerts

Open-source R pipelines turn Uganda's routine health facility reports into cleaned, version-controlled datasets. They feed an outbreak detection dashboard used by the National Malaria Elimination Division, and support estimates of parasite rate from test positivity.
